New vs old tax regime, FY 2026-27: which costs less

Every salaried person has to pick a tax regime, and the choice can be worth lakhs. This guide compares the two on real salaries, explains the ₹12.75 lakh zero-tax line, and shows how many deductions the old regime needs before it wins. Rates as of Budget 2026. Updated 25 November 2026.

What Budget 2026 changed

For salaried people, nothing in the rates. Budget 2026 kept the slabs, the rebate, the standard deduction, the surcharge and the 4% cess exactly as they were for FY 2025-26. The new Income-tax Act took effect on 1 April 2026, but the amounts below are the same as last year’s. The new regime is the default; you can choose the old one if it costs you less.

The two regimes in one paragraph each

New regime. Lower rates over wider slabs (nil up to ₹4 lakh, then 5%, 10%, 15%, 20% and 25% in steps of ₹4 lakh, and 30% above ₹24 lakh), a ₹75,000 standard deduction for salaried people, and almost no other deductions. A rebate of up to ₹60,000 means no tax on taxable income up to ₹12 lakh.

Old regime. Higher rates (5% from ₹2.5 lakh, 20% from ₹5 lakh, 30% from ₹10 lakh for those under 60), a ₹50,000 standard deduction, and the familiar deductions: 80C investments, health insurance, home loan interest, NPS, HRA and others. The full slab tables for every age are on the Income Tax Calculator page.

The ₹12.75 lakh line

Under the new regime a salary of up to ₹12,75,000 pays no income tax: the ₹75,000 standard deduction brings it to ₹12 lakh of taxable income, and the rebate covers the tax on that.

Just above the line, the rules make sure a small raise never leaves you worse off. Tax is limited to the income above ₹12 lakh. A salary of ₹12,85,000 (taxable ₹12,10,000) pays ₹10,400 with cess, instead of the ₹63,960 the slabs alone would give. At ₹13,00,000 the tax is ₹26,000.

Tax on common salaries

Total tax with cess, for a salaried person under 60, in three cases: the new regime, the old regime with ₹1.75 lakh of deductions (80C of ₹1.5 lakh and health insurance of ₹25,000), and the old regime with ₹4.25 lakh (add ₹2 lakh of home loan interest and ₹50,000 of NPS):

How many deductions the old regime needs

The old regime only wins if your deductions (beyond the standard deduction) are large enough. Roughly how much it takes to break even:

₹10 lakh₹4.5 lakh

₹15 lakh₹5.4 lakh

The usual deductions add up to about ₹4.25 lakh before HRA, so the old regime tends to win only for someone who uses all of them and also has a large HRA exemption. Everyone else should still check, but will usually find the new regime cheaper.

How to decide for yourself

  1. Add up the deductions you will really have this year: 80C (up to ₹1.5 lakh), health insurance, home loan interest (up to ₹2 lakh on a self-occupied house), NPS under 80CCD(1B) (up to ₹50,000), and your HRA exemption.
  2. Compare the total with the break-even figure for your salary above.
  3. Better still, put your exact figures into the calculator, which works out both regimes side by side.
  4. Tell your employer your choice so the right tax is deducted each month. If you have business income, switching between regimes is restricted, so check the rules before you change.

What this leaves out

The figures are for resident salaried individuals under 60 with income below ₹50 lakh, so there is no surcharge. They leave out capital gains, which are taxed at their own rates, and other special income. This guide is arithmetic using the published rates, not tax advice. Your return, or a tax professional, has the final word.
