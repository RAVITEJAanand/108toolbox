Numbers in words: lakh, crore and writing a cheque

Amounts on cheques, agreements and invoices are written twice, in figures and in words, and the two must agree. This guide shows how the Indian lakh and crore system works, how it lines up with million and billion, and how to write a cheque that cannot be altered. Updated 25 November 2026.

Why amounts are written in words

A cheque, a sale deed, a rent agreement and many invoices give an amount twice: once in figures and once in words. Figures are quick to read but easy to change, since a 1 becomes a 7 with one stroke. Words are much harder to alter without it showing. When the two disagree on a cheque, banks generally do not pay it, so getting the words right matters.

How the Indian system groups digits

The Indian system puts a comma after the last three digits and then after every two: 12,34,567. The international system puts a comma after every three: 1,234,567. Both are the same number. The names follow the commas:

Indian figuresIndian wordsInternational words
1,000One thousandOne thousand
1,00,000One lakhOne hundred thousand
10,00,000Ten lakhOne million
1,00,00,000One croreTen million
1,00,00,00,000One hundred croreOne billion
1,00,000 croreOne lakh croreOne trillion

Past a crore, everyday and official writing usually repeats the crore: a large budget figure is said as “one lakh crore”, not with a new word. You may also see lac, an older spelling of lakh. It means the same thing.

Reading a number in words, step by step

Group the digits the Indian way first, then read each group with its name, from the left:

  • 2,50,000: 2 lakh, 50 thousand. In words: two lakh fifty thousand.
  • 12,34,567: 12 lakh, 34 thousand, 567. In words: twelve lakh thirty-four thousand five hundred sixty-seven.
  • 1,05,00,750: 1 crore, 5 lakh, 0 thousand, 750. In words: one crore five lakh seven hundred fifty. A group of zeros is simply left out.

The commonest slip comes from reading international commas with Indian names. 1,000,000 is not “one lakh”: regrouped it is 10,00,000, which is ten lakh.

Switching between lakh, crore and million

  • 1 lakh = 100 thousand = 0.1 million
  • 10 lakh = 1 million
  • 1 crore = 100 lakh = 10 million
  • 100 crore = 1 billion
  • 1 lakh crore = 1 trillion

So ₹2.5 crore is 25 million rupees, and a figure of 3 million is 30 lakh. Moving the decimal point by one place is all most of these conversions need.

Writing the amount on a cheque

  1. Start with the word Rupees, then the amount in words, then the paise if there are any, then the word Only. For ₹2,50,000.50 that is: Rupees Two Lakh Fifty Thousand and Fifty Paise Only.
  2. Start writing right at the beginning of the line, and draw a line through any space left at the end, so nothing can be added.
  3. In the box for figures, write the first digit right next to the rupee sign, with no gap.
  4. Read the words and the figures together once before you sign. They must say the same amount.
  5. If you make a mistake in the amount, write a new cheque rather than correcting it. Banks are reluctant to accept changes to the amount.

The word Only is not decoration. It closes the line, so nobody can add “and ten thousand” after it.

Small points that trip people up

  • Pick one system per document. “Ten lakh” and “one million” are both right, but mixing them in one agreement invites confusion.
  • Paise are two digits. ₹10.5 is ten rupees and fifty paise, not five paise.
  • Hyphens are a matter of style. “Thirty-four” and “thirty four” are both read the same way. What matters is that every digit is accounted for.
