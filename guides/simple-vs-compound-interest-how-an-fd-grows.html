Simple vs compound interest: how an FD really grows

A fixed deposit's rate is only the start of the story. Whether interest compounds, how often, whether you take it out as you go, and what tax and rising prices take away all change what you end up with. This guide works it through on ₹1 lakh at 7%. Updated 25 November 2026.

Two ways interest can be counted

Simple interest is paid only on the amount you put in. ₹1,00,000 at 7% earns ₹7,000 every year, no more and no less, so after 5 years you have ₹1,35,000.

Compound interest is also paid on interest you have already earned. Each period’s interest is added to the balance, and the next period’s interest is worked out on that larger balance. The formula is A = P (1 + r ÷ n)n × t, where r is the yearly rate as a fraction, n is how many times a year interest is added, and t is the number of years.

For one year the difference is a few hundred rupees. Over 20 years compounding gives about ₹1.6 lakh more than simple interest on the same deposit. Compounding is slow at first and strong later, which is why time matters more than anything else.

Why a 7% FD gives slightly more than 7%

Most bank fixed deposits that add interest back to the deposit do it every quarter. At 7% a year, that is 1.75% each quarter, and interest earned in the first quarter starts earning in the second. Over a full year ₹1,00,000 becomes ₹1,07,186, which is the same as 7.19% paid once a year. That figure is often called the effective yield, and it is the fair way to compare two deposits that compound differently.

The frequency matters less than people expect. Going from quarterly to monthly compounding on the 10-year figure above adds only about ₹800. The rate and the length of time do almost all the work.

Cumulative or payout: the choice that decides it

A fixed deposit usually comes in two forms:

  • Cumulative: interest is added to the deposit and paid at the end. This is compound interest.
  • Payout (monthly or quarterly income): interest is paid into your account as it is earned. ₹1,00,000 at 7% pays about ₹1,750 a quarter. Because that interest leaves the deposit, it earns nothing more there, so the deposit itself grows like simple interest.

Payout suits someone who needs a regular income. If you do not need the money until the end, cumulative grows more, unless you invest the payouts yourself somewhere that earns as much.

How long it takes to double

The rule of 72 gives a quick estimate: divide 72 by the yearly rate. At 7% that is about 10.3 years. The exact figure for 7% compounded quarterly is just under 10 years. With simple interest the same money takes about 14.3 years to double, because the interest never grows.

What tax and rising prices do to the rate

Interest from a bank deposit is taxed as income, at your own slab rate, and the bank may deduct tax at source once your interest crosses a yearly limit. Prices also rise each year. Together they can shrink a good-looking rate a lot:

  • A 7% deposit with prices rising 6% a year leaves you about 0.9% better off in real terms.
  • If that interest is taxed at 30% (before cess), you keep 4.9%, and with prices rising 6% you are about 1% worse off in real terms each year.

This is not a reason to avoid deposits, which are safe and predictable, but it is a reason to judge them by what is left after tax and inflation, not by the headline rate. Tax rules and limits change, so check the current ones before you rely on them. This guide is general education, not advice for your situation.
