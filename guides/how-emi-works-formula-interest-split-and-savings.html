How EMI works: the formula, the interest split and what saves money

An EMI looks like one simple number, but most of what you pay in the early years is interest, and small changes in tenure, rate or an extra payment move the total by lakhs. This guide works through a ₹30 lakh loan in full. Updated 25 November 2026.

What an EMI is

An EMI, an equated monthly instalment, is one fixed payment you make every month until a loan is cleared. It is not the loan divided by the number of months. It is worked out so that every payment is the same, while the share of each payment that is interest and the share that repays the loan change month by month.

The formula

With P as the amount borrowed, n as the number of monthly instalments and r as the monthly rate (the annual rate divided by 12, then by 100):

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

An 8.5% loan has a monthly rate of about 0.00708, not 8.5. Using the annual figure by mistake is the most common slip when people try this by hand.

A worked example

Take a loan of ₹30,00,000 at 8.5% for 20 years. Here the monthly rate is 0.085 ÷ 12, and there are 240 instalments. The EMI works out to about ₹26,035 a month.

Over 240 months that is ₹62,48,327 paid in total, so the interest alone is ₹32,48,327. On a loan of ₹30 lakh you pay back about ₹62.5 lakh, more than twice what you borrowed.

Why early instalments are mostly interest

Each month the interest is charged on what you still owe. At the start you owe the whole ₹30 lakh, so the first month’s interest is 30,00,000 × 0.085 ÷ 12 = ₹21,250. The EMI is ₹26,035, so only about ₹4,785 of your first payment reduces the loan. Here is how the same EMI splits later:

InstalmentInterest in itLoan repaid by itBalance left after it
1st₹21,250₹4,785₹29,95,215
60th (year 5)₹18,778₹7,256₹26,43,815
120th (year 10)₹14,952₹11,083₹20,99,815
180th (year 15)₹9,108₹16,926₹12,68,962
240th (last)₹183₹25,852₹0

In the first five years you pay about ₹15.6 lakh in EMIs, of which about ₹12.1 lakh is interest and only ₹3.6 lakh reduces the loan. After five years you still owe ₹26.4 lakh, 88% of what you borrowed. This is why closing or moving a loan early does not free as much money as people expect.

What tenure changes

A longer loan lowers the monthly payment and raises the total cost. For the same ₹30 lakh at 8.5%:

TenureEMITotal interestInterest as a share of the loan
15 years₹29,542₹23.18 lakh77%
20 years₹26,035₹32.48 lakh108%
25 years₹24,157₹42.47 lakh142%

Going from 20 to 25 years lowers the EMI by about ₹1,900 a month but adds about ₹10 lakh of interest. A lower monthly figure is easy to accept; the cost of it is in the last column.

What the interest rate changes

For ₹30 lakh over 20 years:

RateEMITotal interest
8.0%₹25,093₹30.22 lakh
8.5%₹26,035₹32.48 lakh
9.0%₹26,992₹34.78 lakh

Each half a percentage point moves the total by about ₹2.3 lakh on this loan. A rate that looks only slightly lower is worth asking about.

What an extra payment does

Money paid early goes against the loan itself and removes all the future interest that amount would have caused. Suppose you pay one extra EMI, ₹26,035, at the end of each year on the ₹30 lakh loan. The loan then ends after about 16 years and 9 months instead of 20, and the total interest falls from ₹32.48 lakh to about ₹26.31 lakh, a saving of about ₹6.2 lakh.

Whether prepaying is possible without a charge depends on the type of loan and the lender’s terms, so check that before you rely on it.

Flat rate and reducing rate are not the same

Some loans quote a flat rate. Interest is then worked out on the full amount borrowed for the whole term, and the loan is not treated as shrinking as you repay it. A ₹1,00,000 loan for 12 months at 10% flat means ₹10,000 of interest, so you pay ₹1,10,000 in 12 instalments of about ₹9,167.

A ₹1,00,000 loan at 10% on a reducing balance has an EMI of about ₹8,792. To get an EMI of ₹9,167 on a reducing balance you would need a rate of about 18%. The two loans look alike on the quote and are not alike. When two offers are quoted differently, convert both to the same basis before comparing them.

What these figures leave out

They are the pure arithmetic of the loan. A real loan may add a processing fee, insurance, or other charges, and a floating rate can change during the term, which changes the EMI or the length of the loan. This guide is general education, not advice about a loan you are considering. Check the exact figures with your lender before you commit.
